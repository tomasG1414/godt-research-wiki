# Common-Path Shearing Interferometry

Self-referencing holography scheme: instead of a separate reference arm, the object beam is interfered with a transversely-shifted ("sheared") copy of itself, generated by e.g. diffraction gratings or dedicated shearing modules. Captured intensity:

$$H(x,y) = |u(x,y) + u(x+\Delta,y)|^2$$

## Advantages

Beams travel near-identical paths → optomechanical robustness, compactness, mutual cancellation of replicated distortions, high stability. Compatible with low-coherence sources since optical path difference between the two beams is small (bounded by shear Δ), unlike off-axis DHM which needs a full separate reference path and thus high temporal coherence.

## Two operating regimes

- **Total shear**: Δ large enough that the two copies are essentially non-overlapping/fully resolved — standard self-interference hologram, phase retrieved directly. Requires a sample-free region to act as reference, so fails on dense/confluent samples where beam copies always overlap with sample structure. Needs high-temporal-coherence source (large OPD).
- **Gradient (small shear)**: Δ → 0, fringe phase becomes a finite-difference approximation of the object's phase *derivative* in the shear direction ($\varphi_\Delta = \Delta\,d\varphi/dx$, via the limit definition of a derivative). This is the regime [[gradient-phase-imaging]] techniques and [[2411-08423-godt|GODT]] operate in. Small OPD tolerates/benefits from low-coherence LED illumination (better SNR, less speckle), and does not require a sample-free region — works on confluent/dense samples.

The UiT/Warsaw instruments name a third label, **partial shear**, for the middle of this range — shear comparable to the diffraction-limited resolution, still gradient-like but not asymptotically small. [[2605-04297-double-sagnac-ls-qpm]] measured what sets its bounds experimentally: above the optimum, shear "exceeds the effective diffraction-limited sampling" and resolution degrades; below it, contrast collapses toward zero shear as the two copies cancel. That is a genuine optimum rather than a "smaller is better" limit, which is worth holding alongside the $\Delta\to0$ idealisation the gradient regime is usually described by. The LS-ODT organoid line (arXiv 2601.20082) uses the same partial-shear framing.

## Hardware: polarization-grating shearing module

[[2411-08423-godt]]'s implementation: two identical polarization diffraction gratings (PG1, PG2) split the object beam into two opposite-circular-polarization copies at diffraction angle $\theta=\sin^{-1}(\lambda/\Gamma)$, recombine them parallel; shear set by grating separation $z$: $\Delta_M = 2z\tan\theta$. Phase-shifting for demodulation achieved by rotating a linear polarizer before the camera (rotation α → 2α phase shift, exploiting the opposite circular polarizations). Same hardware switches between total-shear and gradient mode by adjusting grating separation and coherence of the source — confirmed as a shared, actively-developed platform: [[2024-06-14-598984-polarization-hilbert-holotomography]] runs the identical PG hardware in total-shear mode (as CP-HHT) for lipid-droplet quantification.

## Hardware: cascaded Sagnac shearing module

An alternative to polarization gratings, from [[2605-04297-double-sagnac-ls-qpm]]. One Sagnac interferometer — polarizing beam splitter plus two mirrors — splits the beam by polarization, sends the two components in opposite senses around the loop, and recombines them with a lateral offset set by mirror tilt. Two such loops in series at orthogonal orientations, separated by a 45° polarizer that resets the polarization basis, give four sheared wavefronts carrying both $\partial\phi/\partial x$ and $\partial\phi/\partial y$. Fine shear adjustment is by translating the camera.

Two properties differ from the PG module in ways that matter. Shear is set by **mirror alignment** rather than grating separation, so it is continuously tunable but also drifts and — as that paper measured — ends up unequal between the two axes and non-uniform across the field of view from tube-lens distortion. And the module is built from stock catalogue optics retrofitted to a bright-field microscope's output port, rather than requiring fabricated polarization gratings. The [[godt]]-relevant limitation is that the polarization multiplexing supports exactly two cascaded stages; see [[multi-shear-acquisition]].

### Phase-demodulation alternatives

- **Temporal phase shifting (TPS)**: 4-5 frames at different polarizer rotations; highest accuracy, most frames needed. Used by [[2411-08423-godt]] (5 frames) and as the reference/highest-quality method in [[2024-06-14-598984-polarization-hilbert-holotomography]] (4 frames).
- **Fourier transform (FT) method**: single shot, but requires the object's spectral term to be cleanly separable from its conjugate/autocorrelation terms — breaks down under high-NA conical tomographic illumination because the required Fourier-filter size to capture full NA conflicts with the carrier-frequency shift induced by oblique illumination, forcing a resolution/field-of-view tradeoff. [[2605-04297-double-sagnac-ls-qpm]] shows FT demodulation working single-shot on a *two-carrier mesh* pattern (both shear directions at once) — but under on-axis illumination, so it does not test the tomographic obstacle. Its contribution is showing that low *spatial* coherence is no barrier to carrier demodulation provided temporal coherence is preserved; see [[pseudo-thermal-illumination]].
- **Hilbert spiral transform (HST)** ([[2024-06-14-598984-polarization-hilbert-holotomography]]): single shot, tolerates *overlapping* spectral orders where FT cannot — gives comparable quality to 4-frame TPS at 4× faster acquisition. Not yet confirmed whether compatible with GODT's small-shear/gradient demodulation regime specifically (only demonstrated in total-shear CP-HHT).

## Relevance

Per [[godt]]: this is the physical measurement layer GODT's theory (see [[rytov-approximation]]) is built to consume. A multi-shear reconstruction extension would need multiple shear directions/values from this same class of hardware (or simulated equivalent).
