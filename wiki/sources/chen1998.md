# Validity of Diffraction Tomography Based on the First Born and the First Rytov Approximations

**Citation:** B. Chen and J. J. Stamnes, "Validity of diffraction tomography based on the first Born and the first Rytov approximations," Applied Optics 37(14), 2996–3006 (1998).

**Gist:** Settles (via controlled computer simulation on circular cylinders with exactly-computable exact scattering, compared to filtered-backpropagation reconstructions) a long-running dispute about whether the first [[rytov-approximation|Rytov]] approximation actually has a wider validity range than first Born for diffraction tomography. Answer: yes, by a factor of ~3, and both ranges scale with the product of refractive-index difference and object size — but Rytov's advantage is capped by phase-unwrapping breakdown once its criterion is violated, whereas Born degrades more gracefully.

## Key results

- Quantitative validity criteria, derived from reconstruction error $E_n\% \le 25\%$ on circular-cylinder test objects of radius $a$, refractive-index difference $\delta n = |n-n_0|$, wavelength $\lambda$:
  - First Born (via the plain filtered-backpropagation, FBP, algorithm): $a\,\delta n \le 0.08\lambda$.
  - First Rytov (via the "hybrid FBP" algorithm — requires exact backpropagation of the measured field to the image-area center before forming the Rytov data): $a\,\delta n \le 0.24\lambda$ — **3× larger** than Born's range.
  - This directly contradicts Slaney et al. (1984), who had claimed Rytov validity is essentially size-independent (bounded only by $\delta n<2\%$) and that Born can outperform Rytov for small objects; Chen & Stamnes' simulations show Rytov consistently ≥ Born once scattering isn't vanishingly weak, and both scale with $a\,\delta n$, not $\delta n$ alone.
  - Concrete example: at object radius $a\approx80\lambda$, Rytov-based hybrid FBP requires $\delta n < 0.3\%$ to stay valid.
- Mechanistic origin of Rytov's advantage: writing $u_{sc}^B \equiv u_i\,\delta\chi_R$ (Born scattered field ≡ incident field times Rytov complex-phase perturbation) and Taylor-expanding, $u_R = u_i\exp(\delta\chi_R) \approx u_i(1+u^B_{sc}/u_i+\tfrac{1}{2}(u^B_{sc}/u_i)^2+\dots)$ shows the first-Rytov field implicitly retains higher-order terms that first-Born truncates after the first term — this is why Rytov tolerates larger perturbations before breaking down.
- **Rytov's failure mode is different from Born's**: once the Rytov validity criterion is violated, reconstruction quality collapses abruptly (e.g. $E_n\%$ jumping from 14% to 159% between adjacent test cases) because of **phase-unwrapping breakdown** — the criterion is really a proxy for "adjacent-sample phase difference stays below π." Born-based FBP degrades more gradually as its criterion is exceeded. Practical implication: better phase-unwrapping algorithms, not just better hardware, could extend Rytov's usable range further.
- For very weak scattering (small $a\,\delta n$), both approximations give excellent and near-identical reconstructions — the two only diverge once scattering strength grows.

## Method

2D problem: infinite circular cylinder, normal-incidence plane wave illumination, 25 equally-spaced view angles. Exact scattered field via Hankel-function eigenfunction expansion (closed form for constant-index cylinders); approximate fields via first Born (direct integral, evaluated with the Stamnes–Spjelkavik–Pedersen method) and first Rytov (via the hybrid FBP algorithm, which requires exact backpropagation before Rytov-transforming the data — plain Rytov FBP without this step is *not* what's evaluated). Reconstruction accuracy scored by $E_n\%$ (RMS percent error of reconstructed real refractive index against ground truth over the object footprint), threshold 25% for "success."

## Relevance

Per [[godt]]: this is the quantitative backing for [[2411-08423-godt]]'s and the whole [[optical-diffraction-tomography]] literature's default choice of Rytov over Born. GODT's assumption that Rytov is "less strict" is quantified: a 3× factor, verified in simulation, with an important caveat: that advantage is realized via the *hybrid* FBP algorithm (exact backpropagation step included), not naive Rytov FBP, and it is bounded by phase-unwrapping robustness rather than a smooth degradation. Since GODT works with the phase *gradient* of the shearing signal rather than the wrapped phase itself, it may be structurally less exposed to the phase-unwrapping failure mode this paper identifies as Rytov's Achilles' heel — worth flagging as a candidate explanation for GODT's robustness in dense/thick samples, though no ingested source directly tests this connection *(inference)*.
